Ain Jalut — *the Spring of Goliath* — was fought on 3 September 1260 in Galilee, and it is
usually described as the moment Mongol expansion west finally stopped. That is close to
right, but the interesting part is how close the Mongols came first.

## How It Went Wrong for the Mongols

By the mid-thirteenth century the Mongol empire had fractured into khanates. **Hulagu** had
taken Baghdad in 1258, ending the Abbasid caliphate in the west. That campaign was
extraordinary in its brutality — the Abbasid caliph was killed, the city devastated, and
Persian scholars and physicians massacred at Nishapur.

Then the Mongols overreached. They had never intended to rule Egypt, and they had no
arrangement with its ruler. **Kitbuqa**, commanding for Hulagu, pushed on into Syria anyway,
reducing Aleppo and Damascus in early 1260. Those cities had already submitted, and the
siege of Damascus ended with the Mongols executing the city's governor and a large number of
the population. The message to the Mamluks was unambiguous: submission was not enough.

## The Mamluks Refused to Submit

The **Mamluk Sultanate** was an unusual state — a ruling military caste of slave-soldiers who
had overthrown their own Ayyubid employers, and who were now defending Egypt against an
invasion their own predecessors had invited into the region by displacing the Mongols' former
clients.

They marched north to meet Kitbuqa. The decisive phase was fought at **Ain Jalut**, where the
Mamluk vanguard was commanded by **Baybars**, who used the local geography well. The Mongol
army was defeated, its commander killed, and the survivors retreated back across the Euphrates.

The aftermath matters more than the battle. The Mongols withdrew from Syria entirely, and in
1261 a treaty was concluded in Cairo between the Ilkhanate and the Mamluks. Neither side could
absorb the other, and they coexisted on the frontier for the next century and a half.

## Why It Matters

- **It broke the myth of Mongol invincibility.** The Mongols had destroyed an empire far more
  powerful and ancient than Egypt — the Abbasid caliphate had outlasted Rome by centuries.
  They had never previously been stopped by another state in open field battle.
- **It preserved Mamluk Egypt** and, downstream, the political survival of the Mamluks as a
  dynasty.
- **It set the frontier** between the Mamluk Sultanate and the Ilkhanate that shaped Levantine
  politics for 150 years.
- It is a useful corrective to the popular image of the Mongols as an unstoppable force. They
  were one of the most capable field armies in history, and they still lost when overextended.

## The Sources

This battle is unusually well documented for the Mongol period, because both sides wrote.

- **Ibn Khaldun** was close to the Mamluk court and his account, though written later, is the
  most vivid Western treatment.
- **Rashid al-Din**, the Persian vizier of the Ilkhanate, compiled the *Jami' al-Tawarikh*
  (Compendium of Chronicles) at enormous scale — the other major narrative source.
- Egyptian Mamluk chronicles and the continuing official Mamluk historiography give the victors'
  version.
- Modern scholarship has benefited from access to both Persian and Arabic sources, as well as
  Chinese and Mongolian records, which earlier generations of historians did not have.

One caution when reading any of them: all of it is political. The Mongols were writing for a
court that had just committed a massacre and needed it justified, and the Mamluks were writing
a victory narrative. Neither side is neutral about 1258.
